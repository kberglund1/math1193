\begin{problem}
Solve \(4x - 5y = 10\) for \(x\). Now solve it for \(y\).

\begin{explanation}
When we say solve an equation for \(x\), we mean isolate \(x\) on one side.

Here's some work:
\begin{align*}
    4x - 5y & = 10 \\
    4x & = 10 - 5y \\
    x & = \frac{10 - 5y}{4} \\
    x & = -\frac{5}{4}y + \frac{5}{2}
\end{align*}

The last line is not strictly necessary, but may be a nicer way to write it.

Solving for \(y\):
\begin{align*}
    4x - 5y & = 10 \\
    -5y & = 10 - 4x \\
    y & = \frac{10 -4x}{-5} \\
    y & = -2 + \frac{4}{5}x
\end{align*}
\end{explanation}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Solve \(0.01x + 0.5y = 1\) for \(x\). Now solve it for \(y\).
Share with a group how you solved the equation. See if you can come up with more ways. Can you use fractions? Whole numbers?

\begin{explanation}
When we say solve an equation for \(x\), we mean isolate \(x\) on one side.

Here's some work:
\begin{align*}
    0.01x + 0.5y & = 1 \\
    0.01x & = 1 - 0.5y \\
    x & = \frac{1 - 0.5y}{0.01}
\end{align*}

We could also convert everything to fractions:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{x}{100} + \frac{y}{2} & = 1 \\
    100\left(\frac{x}{100} + \frac{y}{2}\right) & = 100 \cdot 1 \\
    x + 50y & = 100 \\
    x = 100 - 50y
\end{align*}

Decimals are similar.

Solving for \(y\):
\begin{align*}
    0.01x + 0.5y & = 1 \\
    0.5y & = 1 - 0.01x \\
    y & = \frac{1 -0.01x}{0.5} \\
    y & = \frac{1}{0.5} - \frac{0.01x}{0.5} \\
    y & = \frac{1}{1/2} - \frac{x/100}{1/2} \\
    y & = 1 \cdot \frac{2}{1} - \frac{x}{100}\cdot \frac{2}{1} \\
    y & = 2 - \frac{x}{50}
\end{align*}

You should be comfortable working with decimals, fractions, and converting between them.
\end{explanation}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Let \(2a + b = 23\) and \(a = -b + 1\). 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rewrite the first equation in terms of \(b\). That is, rewrite it so no terms contain the variable \(a\).
    \item Rewrite the second equation in terms of \(a\). That is, rewrite it so no terms contain the variable \(b\).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{explanation}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Since \(a = -b + 1\), we can substitute into the first equation:
        \begin{align*}
            2(-b + 1) + b & = 23 \\
            -2b + 2 + b & = 23 \\
            -b + 2 & = 23 \\
            -b & = 21 \\
            b & = -21
        \end{align*}
        \item Now, from the first equation, \(b = 23 - 2a\). Substituting, we find that
        \begin{align*}
            a & = -(23 - 2a) + 1 \\
            a & = -23 + 2a + 1 \\
            a & = -22 + 2a \\
            a - 2a & = -22 \\
            -a & = -22 \\
            a & = 22
        \end{align*}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{explanation}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Graph the equations \(x + y = 2\) and \(x + y = 3\)
    without a calculator. Do these lines ever touch? Why or why not?
    Can you find an \(x\) and a \(y\) that satisfy both equations at 
    the same time? Why or why not? 
    \item Graph the equations \(x + y = 2\) and \(x - y = 3\)
    without a calculator. Do these lines ever touch? Why or why not?
    Can you find an \(x\) and a \(y\) that satisfy both equations at 
    the same time? Why or why not? 
    \item Is it possible for two lines in the 
    plane to touch more than once? Debate with a friend.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{explanation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Notice that the two equations are not in slope-intercept form (that is, \(y = mx + b\)), so one approach is to rearrange them into 
    that form: \(y = - x + 2\) and \(y = - x + 3\). Now you can see that these are lines with slope \(-1\) and \(y\)-intercepts 2 and 3, respectively.

    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=10cm]{parallelLines.png}
    \end{center}

    From their graphs, these lines are parallel, so they do not ever touch.

    There are a few ways to think about the problem of finding an \(x\) and \(y\) that satisfy both equations at the same time. 
    
    Graphically, since the two lines don't intersect, there isn't an \((x, y)\) pair that lies on both of the graphs. But \((x, y)\) pairs
    are only on the graphs if they satisfy the equations and vice versa. Therefore, no \(x\) and \(y\) can satisfy both equations at the same time.

    You can also think about this by substituting. Since any \(y\) that satisfies both equations has to equal both \(-x + 2\) and \(-x + 3\) at the same time,
    by the transitive property of equality, \(-x + 2 = -x + 3\). Canceling the \(-x\), we arrive at the conclusion that \(2 = 3\), which
    can't be true. Therefore, no such \(y\) exists.

    \item Notice that the two equations are not in slope-intercept (\(y = mx + b\)) form, so one approach is to rearrange them into 
    that form: \(y = - x + 2\) and \(y = x - 3\). Now you can see that these are lines with slope \(-1\) and \(1\) and \(y\)-intercepts 2 and 3, respectively.

    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=10cm]{perpLines.png}
    \end{center}

    From their graphs, these lines are not parallel, so they will touch. In fact, they're perpendicular, so they cross at a right angle.

    There are a few ways to think about the problem of finding an \(x\) and \(y\) that satisfy both equations at the same time. 
    
    Graphically, since the two lines intersect, there's an \((x, y)\) pair that lies on both of the graphs. But \((x, y)\) pairs
    are only on the graphs if they satisfy the equations and vice versa. Therefore, some \(x\) and \(y\) can satisfy both equations at the same time.

    You can also think about this by substituting. Since any \(y\) that satisfies both equations has to equal both \(-x + 2\) and \(x - 3\) at the same time,
    by the transitive property of equality, \(-x + 2 = x - 3\). Solving this equation gives us \(x = \frac{5}{2}\). Plugging into one of the original
    equations, we see that \(y = \frac{5}{2} - 3\), or \(y = -\frac{1}{2}\). This means that \(\left(\frac{5}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\right)\) is
    an \((x, y)\) pair that satisfies both equations at the same time.

    \item Two lines that have more than one intersection point must be the same line, since
    there's only one line that connects any two points in the plane. As soon as you choose two coordinate points, you've automatically
    determined your unique line.

    Note that this doesn't mean there's only one equation, since the same line can be represented by different equations.
\end{enumerate}
\end{explanation}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Your second-favorite music artist is playing at a concert venue. There are two types of tickets. VIP seating costs \$100, and general admission costs \$40. 
Ticket sales totaled \$53,000. Your friend who works at the venue tells you they sold 50 VIP seats.

How many general admission tickets were sold?


\begin{explanation}
Let's define some variables. Let \(v\) be the number of VIP tickets sold, and let \(g\) be the number of general admission (GA)
tickets sold. Our goal, as stated by the problem, is to find \(g\). Actually, the problem statement tells us outright that \(v = 50\).

\begin{align*}
    (\text{revenue from GA ticket sales}) + (\text{revenue from VIP ticket sales}) & = (\text{total revenue}) \\
     (\text{revenue per GA ticket sold}) \cdot (\text{number of GA tickets sold}) & \\ + (\text{revenue per VIP ticket sold}) \cdot (\text{number of VIP tickets sold}) & = 53000 \\
    40 \cdot g + 100 \cdot v & = 53000 \\
    40g + 100 \cdot 50 & = 53000 \\
    40g +5000 & = 53000 \\
    40g & = 48000 \\
    g & = 1200
\end{align*}

So they sold 1200 general admission tickets.

You could have done this without defining variables, but as problems get more complicated, it's good to be able to keep
track of things so you can reference them later and explain your answers to others.
\end{explanation}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Leo has a solution that is 2\% bleach by volume (that is, for every 100mL, 2mL is bleach and 98mL is water). He also has one that is 5\% bleach by volume. 

How much of each solution (in mL) should Leo use if he wants to make 500mL of a 4\% bleach solution?

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Define variables that help you solve this 
    problem. Be specific about what quantities the variables
    represent. Compare with a friend and discuss why you made 
    the choices you made.
    \item Write equations that relate the variables you 
    have defined.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{explanation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Call the 2\% bleach solution Solution X, the 5\% bleach solution Solution Y, and the 4\% bleach solution Solution Z. Let \(x\) be the amount in mL of Solution X Leo should use and let \(y\) be the amount in mL of Solution Y
    that Leo should use. 
    \item The amount of Solution X used and the amount of Solution Y used add up to the total
    amount of Solution Z produced. Remember that Leo wants to produce 500mL of Solution Z.
    Translating into our variables we defined, this can
    be expressed by the equation \(x + y = 500\).

    Now we can write an equation describing the bleach content of each solution. 
    Since Solution Z is a 4\% solution, and we end up with 500mL of Solution Z,
    the total amount of bleach is \(0.04 \cdot 500 = 20\) mL. Since Solution X is a 2\% solution,
    and Leo uses \(x\) mL of Solution X, there is \(0.02\cdot x\) mL of bleach in the Solution X used. Similarly, there is
    \(0.05 \cdot y\) mL of bleach in the Solution Y used. The amount of bleach in the Solution X used and the amount of bleach in the
    Solution Y used should add up to the amount of bleach in the Solution Z
    produced, so \(0.02x + 0.05y = 20\).

    I'm being very explicit with where each equation is coming from, and you should be able to understand what each part of the equations 
    corresponds to in the real-world problem.
\end{enumerate}

\end{explanation}
\end{problem}
