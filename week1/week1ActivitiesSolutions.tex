\begin{problem}
    Factor the polynomial \(x^2 - x - 6\). Compare with 
your group and make sense of why each of your methods
works. If you all used the same method, try to find
a different one.

Now solve the equation \(x^2 - x - 6 = 0\).

\begin{instructorNotes}
The AC method has you multiply the coefficient of the \(x^2\) and constant terms.
In this case, \(1 \times (-6) = -6\). Now you need to find two numbers that multiply
to \(-6\) and add to \(-1\), which is the coefficient of the \(x\) term.
Two numbers that work are \(-3\) and \(2\). Then we can write
\begin{align*}
x^2 - x - 6 & = x^2 -3x + 2x - 6 \\
& = (x^2 - 3x) + (2x - 6) \\
& x(x - 3) + 2(x - 3) \\
& = (x - 3)(x + 2)
\end{align*}
Take some time and see if you can understand why each equality is true.

In the first line, since \(-1 = -3 + 2\), \(-x = -3x + 2x\) by combining like terms.

In the second line, we did some grouping with parentheses.

In the third line, we factored an \(x\) out of the first set of parentheses and a 2 out of the second.

In the last line, we did some more complicated factoring. Notice that \((x - 3)\) is a factor of both terms, so 
we can pull it out. We're left with \((x + 2)\).

Our equation then can be rewritten as \((x - 3)(x + 2) = 0\). When two factors multiply to give you \(0\), 
either the first factor must equal \(0\), so \(x - 3 = 0\), or the second must equal \(0\), so \(x + 2 = 0\). 

Solving these two equations yields \(x = 3\) and \(x = -2\) as solutions. To check, you can plug these back into the original equation.
\end{instructorNotes}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Factor the polynomial \(x^2 - 25\). Compare with 
your group and make sense of why each of your methods
works. If you all used the same method, try to find
a different one.

Now solve the equation \(x^2 - 25 = 0\).

\begin{instructorNotes}
The difference (subtraction) of squares method only works for expressions of the form \(a^2 - b^2\), but can be useful to save time. If you have
something that looks like \(a^2 - b^2\), it factors as \((a - b)(a + b)\).

Notice that \(25 = 5^2\), so our polynomial becomes \(x^2 - 5^2\). Because it's a difference of squares, it factors as \((x - 5)(x + 5)\).\

Now like in the previous problem, our equation then can be rewritten as \((x - 5)(x + 5) = 0\). When two factors multiply to give you \(0\), 
either the first factor must equal \(0\), so \(x - 5 = 0\), or the second must equal \(0\), so \(x + 5 = 0\). 

Solving these two equations yields \(x = 5\) and \(x = -5\) as solutions. To check, you can plug these back into the original equation.


\end{instructorNotes}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Solve the inequality \(-2x + 4 \ge 0\). Shade the 
solution set on the number line, then write the 
solution set in interval notation.

\begin{instructorNotes}
When we solve an inequality, we want to find \textbf{all} \(x\)-values that make the inequality true. For example, \(0\) is a solution 
to the inequality (try testing it!).

One solution could be
\begin{align*}
    -2x + 4 & \ge 0 \\
    -2x & \ge -4 \\
    x & \le 2
\end{align*}
Take some time and see if you understand each step.

In the first step, we subtracted \(4\) from both sides of the inequality. 

In the second, we divided by \(-2\). Notice that when we divide or multiply by a negative number on both sides of an inequality,
the inequality changes from \(\ge\) to \(\le\). This also happens with \(\le\) to \(\ge\), \(<\) to \(>\) and \(>\) to \(<\).

The number line looks like this. All numbers to the left of 2 are shaded, since \(x \le 2\). The underline on \(\le\) means that 2 itself is included
in the solution set, so 2 is shaded with a closed dot.

\begin{problem}
Jaden was given the following problem:

\textit{Let \(f(x) = x^2 + x + 1\). Find \(f(a + 2)\).}

Jaden's work is as follows:

\begin{align*}
    f(a + 2) & = a + 2^2 + a + 2 + 1 \\
    & = a + 4 + a + 3 \\
    & = 2a + 7

Is Jaden's work correct? Why or why not? Where did he 
go wrong? What did he do correctly?
